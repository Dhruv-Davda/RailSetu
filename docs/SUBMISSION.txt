HOW WELL DID WE IMPLEMENT THE TASK, AND WHY IS THIS THE BEST WAY TO DO IT?
Challenge #985: Policy Simulation, Change History


(1) FIRST WE HAD TO DECIDE WHAT A "POLICY CHANGE" ACTUALLY IS

(i) The obvious reading is a code change. Someone edits a file, you diff it, you show the diff. We threw that out within the hour, because nobody in a railway control room reads a pull request.

(ii) The changes that matter on a railway are decisions: at what density do we call a location a crush point, how long may a dispatcher hold a train so another can pass, what coverage counts as protected. Those are the numbers argued over in a safety review and signed off by a department. Those are policy.

(iii) So we went looking for them in our own MVP, and found them scattered through the models as bare literals. A 5.0 sat in the crowd simulation. A 12.0 sat in the corridor engine. Somebody on our team had typed each of those, and three weeks later none of us could say who, or why, or what we would have to change to put it back.

(iv) That was the moment this stopped being a hackathon add-on. We were not going to build a policy layer. We were going to take the one that already existed and drag it into the daylight.


(2) WE TURNED THE RULES INTO DOCUMENTS

Eight of them: six structured rule-sets the models read directly, and two written standards in prose that govern what staff do. Each carries a department, a filename, and a version history of its own.

(i) They are text, not a settings form. A form gives you a database write. Text gives you something that diffs line by line, carries comments explaining each rule, and reads like an amendment rather than a config change. When a duty officer opens the change log, we want them looking at something resembling the document they signed off, not a row that changed in a table.

(ii) They are eight documents, not one rulebook. A railway does not amend crowd thresholds and train precedence in the same act. Different people own them, they change on different cycles, and a reader wants the history of one of them without wading through edits to the others. So each has its own version chain, and the platform runs on all of them composed together.

(iii) We were equally deliberate about what we refused to put in. Walking speed, the simulation timestep, the node catchment areas: those are empirical or numerical. Nobody decides them. Including them would have turned a policy register into an arbitrary config editor, and the moment you can edit anything, the register stops meaning anything.


(3) THE HARD PART: PREVIEWING A CHANGE EXACTLY

This is what the brief asks for, and it is where a project either does the real thing or fakes it.

(i) The easy path is a second, simplified calculator: a "what-if" estimator that approximates what the change would do. We have both watched that pattern rot. The estimator and the real model drift apart over a few sprints, and one day the preview says one thing and production does another, which is worse than having no preview at all.

(ii) So we did not write a second model. We made the real one answerable to a different question. In our code it reads: "with use_policy(draft): preview = run_everything()". Real models, draft rules, nothing persisted.

(iii) The simulators never take a policy argument threaded down through a dozen call signatures. They ask for the rules in force, and a preview runs the same code under a different answer.

(iv) That buys us exactness. The preview is not an approximation of the real simulation. It is the real simulation with one value swapped.

(v) It also buys us safety under concurrency. The override lives in a context variable rather than a global, so it is scoped to the block that set it, and one person previewing a draft cannot alter what another person's request computes. With a module-level global, previewing would have been a production-affecting act.

(vi) The result: hit preview, and the crowd, corridor and protection models all run twice, once under the policy in force and once under your draft. You are shown the deltas before anything is written.


(4) WHAT A CHANGE HAS TO CARRY TO BE WORTH RECORDING

(i) Identity. A register that says "someone changed something" is not a register, so the tab requires a sign-in. The address becomes the identity, and it flows through every push, revert and restore.

(ii) Location. We record where a change was pushed from, because on a railway that is not incidental: an amendment raised from the station it governs reads differently from one raised from head office.

(iii) Honest absence, which is the choice we are most pleased with. If the browser refuses permission, or the device cannot fix a position, the version records that plainly. We could have guessed a location from the IP address and shown a city name. We deliberately did not. A register that fabricates evidence is worse than one that admits a gap.

(iv) Immutability. Every activated version is permanent. Correcting a bad policy means writing a new version, never editing history, because the record of what was in force at a given moment has to survive the decision to change it.


(5) UNDO, DONE PROPERLY

This is the part we are proudest of, and where we spent the most time.

(i) Most projects would ship one button: restore this version. We shipped two, because git has known for twenty years that they are different acts. Restore makes the document exactly as it was and discards everything since. Revert backs out only what that version introduced and keeps every later change, which is almost always what someone means by "that change was wrong".

(ii) Revert is a real reverse patch. We take the diff a version introduced and apply it backwards to the text currently in force, matching each block with a line of surrounding context so a pure deletion has something to anchor to, and splicing back to front so earlier indices stay valid.

(iii) If a block cannot be found because a later amendment already touched those lines, we report a conflict naming what we expected and what we would have written. We do not guess.

(iv) We also refuse a revert that would leave the document invalid, and re-validate before anything is written. A register that silently mangles a safety threshold would be worse than one that says it cannot do this automatically. That refusal is a feature.


(6) HOW WE KNOW IT WORKS

(i) The claim underneath all of this is that these documents genuinely drive the platform, rather than sitting decoratively beside it. So we proved it. With the source tree's fingerprint byte-for-byte identical, we changed the crush threshold from 5.0 to 3.6, and the crowd model reported a fifth crush point under the same surge. The platform behaved differently because the rules changed. Nothing else did.

(ii) We tested it like something that has to survive contact with real users: 755 checks across fourteen suites, covering conflicts, no-ops, reverts that would produce an invalid document, prose documents, permission refused on location, and the whole thing driven through a real browser.

(iii) The suites assert properties rather than memorised numbers. Narrowing the overtake window in policy must make the dispatcher stop taking those overtakes. That holds whatever the timetable is, and fails the moment the register stops actually governing anything.


(7) WHY WE THINK THIS IS THE BEST WAY TO DO IT

(i) It fits where the brief asked it to fit. It is not a tab bolted onto the side of the product. It is the layer the rest of the product now stands on, and it works the same way whether the theme is railways or anything else, exactly as the challenge required.

(ii) It does the difficult version of preview instead of the easy one, and the difficult version is the only one that stays true as the code moves.

(iii) It is honest at every point where being honest was harder: about where a change came from, about what it would break, and about the fact that raising a threshold does not make a station safer, only quieter. It tells you that to your face, before you push.


A rule that nobody can trace is a rule that nobody can be answerable for. That was the whole point.
